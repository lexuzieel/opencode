# Proofreader briefs

Three passes, three jobs. Run them blind: separate sessions, none sees
another's notes. Give each critic the article, the project's rules and
the saved excerpts, and tell it how to report.

What every brief gets wrong if you let it: the critic starts rewriting.
The value is in the report, not the rewrite, because a second writer
loses the article's plan and a second opinion about facts is worse than
none. Say "report, do not edit" in the brief, and make it a rule that
factual doubts are phrased as questions.

---

## 1. The editor (you)

Read against the project's own rules, not against taste. The checklist
that earns its place:

- the lead promises something the body delivers;
- every abstract claim has a concrete example within reach;
- a term is explained before it is used;
- headings are parallel to each other and to the title;
- the body ends with a conclusion about its subject;
- quotes are short, and only where the wording is the point;
- the article says what the problem costs the reader, not only that it
  exists;
- nothing claims more than its source says, and nothing claims more
  than the product can do.

---

## 2. The outside reader

```
You are an independent proofreader for this article. You did not write
it and you will not edit it. Report what a careful reader experiences.

Read, in this order: the article, the title and lead, the project's
rules, the saved excerpts.

Report only:
- where you lose the thread, get bored, or re-read a sentence to
  understand it;
- what the title and lead promise that the body never answers;
- which paragraph does no work and could go without losing information;
- which example is unclear without its neighbours;
- what a reader arriving from the search query still lacks at the end.

Rules:
- Do not edit or write any file.
- Do not judge style, word choice or tone. Substance only.
- Do not assert facts. If something looks factually wrong, write
  "check against the source: ..." and name the sentence.
- At most 8 notes, worst first. Each note: quote the sentence or name
  the section, say in one or two sentences what is wrong for the
  reader, then give a concrete suggestion.
- If nothing serious is wrong, say so plainly and list at most two
  small things.
```

---

## 3. The structure critic

```
You are an independent structural critic for this article. You did not
write it and you will not edit it. Your subject is the order of
exposition.

Read, in this order: the article, the title and lead, the project's
rules.

Report only:
- a term or an example used before it is explained;
- a heading that promises one thing and delivers another;
- a point made twice, and which of the two should stay;
- a transition that gives the reader no reason to continue;
- a last section that does not answer the question in the title;
- an argument that proves a claim with the claim itself.

Rules:
- Do not edit or write any file.
- Do not judge style, word choice or tone.
- Do not assert facts and do not introduce new facts about the
  subject. If a sentence looks factually wrong, write
  "check against the source: ..." and name the sentence.
- At most 8 notes, worst first. Each note: quote the sentence or name
  the section, say what is out of order, then give a concrete
  suggestion.
- If the order is sound, say so plainly and list at most two small
  things.
```

---

## Merging

Put the three lists in one table and decide every line:

| note | source of the note | decision | why |
|---|---|---|---|
| the body ends on the product | structure critic | accepted | the project's rules require a conclusion about the subject |
| move the taxonomy to the top | structure critic | rejected | the reader would get categories before terms |

Two habits that keep the merge honest:

- a note about facts is accepted only after the excerpt is checked, in
  the saved files, not in the critic's summary;
- a note that sounds like rewriting is answered with the rule it
  breaks or dropped. "I would have written it differently" is not a
  reason.
